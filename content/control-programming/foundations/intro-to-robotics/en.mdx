<Section id="robot-definition" n="01" title="What is a robot?">

In everyday language the word **robot** means several different things:

- In science fiction — a machine that *moves* and *behaves* like a human.
- In older British English — a set of automatic traffic lights (*"waiting at the robot"*).
- In South Africa — a colloquial term for a traffic signal.

The formal dictionary definition, however, is more precise:

> **A machine capable of carrying out a complex series of actions automatically,
> especially one programmable by a computer.**

The word itself comes from the Czech word **_robota_** meaning *forced labour*,
coined by Karel Čapek in his 1920 play *R.U.R.* (*Rossum's Universal Robots*).

For robotics — not science fiction — a robot is best understood not as a single
object but as a **system**: a set of tightly coupled mechanical, electrical,
computational, and software subsystems working together to perceive and act on
the physical world.

</Section>

<Section id="mechanics" n="02" title="Robots as mechatronic systems">

Every robot is a **mechatronic system** — it joins mechanics with electronics
and software. The diagram below shows the main subsystems and how they connect:

<Figure src="/intro-to-robotics/robot-systems.png" alt="Robot subsystems diagram" caption="Mechatronic system overview: sensor → controller → actuator → feedback" width="80%" />

> Reference: https://electricalacademia.com/electronics/mechatronic-system-examples/

| Subsystem | What it provides | Typical examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Mechanical** | Structure and motion | Gears, linkages, arms, wheels, end-effectors |
| **Electrical** | Power and signal routing | Battery, motor drivers, wiring harness, PCBs |
| **Sensing** | Information about state and environment | Encoders, IMU, cameras, LiDAR, force sensors |
| **Computational** | Intelligence — the software brain | Microcontroller, SBC, real-time OS |

Data flows from sensors into the computer, and commands flow from the computer
to actuators. Between those two directions sits the **software pipeline** that
turns raw sensor numbers into motion — and that pipeline is the subject of this
course.

> **Key idea:** In a tele-operated robot the human is the planner. In an
> autonomous robot the *software* must sense, plan, and act on its own.

</Section>

<Section id="five-pillars" n="03" title="The five pillars of autonomy">

  <Slide>
    Mobile robot autonomy is a research area that does not belong to a single
    discipline. It draws on **five** major scientific fields, each of which solves a
    distinct sub-problem:

    ### 1. Computer Vision & Signal Processing

    Real-world sensor data is noisy, incomplete, and changes fast. Computer vision
    turns raw pixel grids into objects, lanes, and features. Signal processing
    filters audio, vibration, and electrical noise so the rest of the pipeline
    receives clean, actionable data.

    > 🔜 **Future course preview:** Signal filtering and basic image analysis form
    > the core of the *Perception* topic.

    ### 2. Probability Theory & Estimation

    Sensors are never perfect. Motors slip. The environment changes. Probability
    theory lets the robot **estimate** where it is — and what is happening around
    it — even when every single reading has uncertainty.

    > 🔜 **Future course preview:** State estimation and filters under
    > *Localization / State-Estimation*.

    ### 3. Optimization Theory

    Given a set of constraints and a goal, optimization finds the *best* behaviour.
    Path planning, schedule design, motor torque allocation — they all boil down to
    **"*Find the maximum or minimum of a function.*"**
  </Slide>

  <Slide>
    ### 4. Control Theory

    Planning tells the robot *what* to do. **Control** tells it *how* to do it,
    feedback loop by feedback loop, continuously closing the gap between the plan
    and reality.

    > 🔜 **Future course preview:** The full PID loop, tuning, and code
    > implementation under *Control Theory*.

    ### 5. Machine Learning

    Data is the new calibration. Supervised, self-supervised, unsupervised, and
    reinforcement learning let a robot *improve* over time — without a human
    rewriting its rules by hand.

    > 🔜 **Future course preview:** ML in the context of robot perception and
    > adaptive control.

  </Slide>

</Section>

<Section id="sense-plan-act" n="04" title="Sense → Plan → Act">

All autonomy pipelines — from Mars rovers to autonomous cars to competition
robots — boil down to one loop:

```
[ Sense  ] → [ Plan  ] → [ Act  ] →  (repeat)
   ↑                                     │
   └──────────── feedback ───────────────┘
```

Each **arrow** to the outside world introduces **uncertainty**:

- **Sense → Plan**: raw sensor data is noisy; the robot must interpret it into a coherent model.
- **Plan → Act**: a perfect plan fails if the robot's hardware cannot execute it precisely.
- **Act → Sense**: executing *changes* the world, which must be sensed again.

This is the **sense–plan–act loop**. Every autonomy system on any platform implements
this loop in some form.

> **Why it matters:** The loop's three stages are not rigidly sequential. In some
> robots, sensing, planning, and control run on a single microcontroller at 100+ Hz.
> In others they span different machines (a perception server, a planning server,
> a control board). The *sequence of logic* remains the same regardless.

</Section>

<Section id="perception" n="05" title="Perception — reading the world">

Perception is the study of turning **raw sensor values** into **meaningful
models** the robot (and its software) can reason about.

A camera does not "see" a person. It records a grid of numbers — thousands of
pixels, each an RGB triplet. Perception turns those numbers into the concept:
*"there is a person at position X, moving at velocity V, of class 'human'."*

**Common perception tasks:**
- Object detection (people, goals, targets) from camera images
- Point-cloud processing from LiDAR / sonar for obstacle avoidance
- IMU fusion for orientation and acceleration estimates

> **Key insight:** Perception is often the **processing bottleneck** of an
> autonomy system because raw sensor data is orders of magnitude larger — and
> noisier — than what the planning stage actually needs. Good perception
> compresses noise into reliable abstractions.

</Section>

<Section id="estimation" n="06" title="Localization & State Estimation">

At any moment the robot must answer: *"Given everything I've sensed so far, what
is my best guess about where I am and what is happening around me?"*

This is an **estimation problem**, not a simple measurement problem. Common
approaches include:

| Approach | What it does | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Particle filter** | Maintains many position hypotheses, weights them by sensor match | Accurate in any environment; slow for many particles |
| **Kalman filter** | Recursive estimator optimal for linear systems + Gaussian noise | Fast; breaks down for highly non-linear situations |
| **Visual odometry** | Tracks features between camera frames to estimate motion (Forster et al., RSS 2015) | No dedicated hardware needed; drifts over long distances |

</Section>

<Section id="planning" n="07" title="Planning — finding a path">

Given a goal and a model of the world, planning finds the *best* sequence of
actions to reach it. The simplest case is **shortest path** on a known map:

| Step | Current node | Visited nodes | Time |
|------|-------------|---------------|------|
| 1 | Start (0,0) | {(0,0)} | 0 s |
| 2 | (0, 1) | {(0,0), (0,1)} | 0.02 s |
| 3 | (1, 1) | {(0,0), (0,1), (1,1)} | 0.04 s |
| 4 | Goal (2,2) | … | 0.06 s |

A real robot's planner must also handle things that don't appear on paper —
dynamics (a robot can't instantaneously change velocity), moving obstacles, joint
limits, and the trade-off between optimal path quality and computation time.

> **Key insight:** Planning is about **trade-offs**. The path that looks perfect
> in simulation may fail on a real robot because of slip, sensor noise, and
> latency. Good planning *anticipates* failure.

</Section>

<Section id="control" n="08" title="Control — executing the plan">

A planner might say *"move the arm to 45° in 2 seconds".*
A controller turns that command into **actual** servo movements — and
continuously corrects when reality drifts away from the plan.

**Feedback control** is the machinery that does this: it measures the gap
between where the robot *is* and where the plan says it *should be*, then
applies a corrective action. Repeat.

This is where we meet the **PID controller** — the proportional, integral, and
derivative loop — in a later course. The key takeaway here is that control is
**not optional**: even with a perfect plan and perfect sensors, mechanical
imperfections make feedback indispensable.

</Section>

<Section id="simulation" n="09" title="Simulation — the bridge between real and planned">

Simulation lets engineers verify a perception–planning–control pipeline without
worrying about risking hardware, draining battery cells, or damaging mechanisms.

Modern tools like [**Foxglove Studio**](https://foxglove.dev/) receive real-time sensor messages from the
robot over a WebSocket bridge, rendering them in interactive visual panels.
Engineers can validate each pipeline stage — perception, estimation, planning,
control — in a shared view, side-by-side with live telemetry.

> **Key insight:** Simulation is not a replacement for real-robot testing. It is
> a **bridge** — a way to catch logic errors early, practice new strategies, and
> share data across a team *before* the hardware ever moves.

</Section>
