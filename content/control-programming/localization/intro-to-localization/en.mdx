<Section id="probabilistic-robotics" n="01" title="What is Probabilistic Robotics?" chip="Uncertainty">

<Callout type="info" title="Further Reading / Inspired By">

- [*Probabilistic Robotics*](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262201629/probabilistic-robotics/) by Sebastian Thrun, Wolfram Burgard, and Dieter Fox.
- [*State Estimation for Robotics*](https://asrl.utias.utoronto.ca/~tdb/bib/barfoot_ser17.pdf) by Timothy D. Barfoot.

You don't need to read either book to follow this lesson.

</Callout>

Imagine a competition robot trying to line up for a score. Its map shows the
scoring target, but the map doesn't tell the robot **where it is on that map**.
Figuring out its position and heading — together called its **pose** — is
**localization**. Pose is one example of a robot's **state**: the information we
want to know about it right now.

**Probabilistic robotics** studies algorithms that explicitly account for
uncertainty in both **state estimation** (what's likely true now?) and **control
outcomes** (what's likely to happen if I send this command?). Three things make
that uncertainty unavoidable:

1. **The environment changes.** <Reveal>Someone walks between the robot and a landmark,
   or a lighting change makes the landmark harder for a camera to see.</Reveal>
2. **Sensors are noisy and sometimes misleading.** <Reveal>A distance sensor aimed at a
   stationary wall might flicker between 99 and 101 cm. An encoder can correctly
   count wheel turns even when the wheel slips and the robot barely moves.</Reveal>
3. **Motion is hard to predict.** <Reveal>Command “drive forward 1 meter” and you might
   get 0.98 m on one run and 1.02 m on the next. Battery voltage, floor grip,
   and tiny steering differences all matter; exactly 1.000 m is unlikely.</Reveal>

Rather than pretending one reading or command tells the whole story, a robot
can keep track of several possible answers and how plausible each one is.

</Section>

<Section id="estimation-history" n="02" title="A Brief History of Estimation & Localization" chip="History">

<Slide>

### From missing planets to landing on the Moon

- **1801 — Gauss and least squares.** At just 24, Carl Friedrich Gauss took on an
  astronomical estimation problem: where would the dwarf planet Ceres reappear
  after being lost in the Sun's glare? About nine months after the last sighting,
  his prediction was within half a degree. Gauss developed **least squares**, which
  chooses a prediction by making the sum of squared measurement mismatches as
  small as possible. In 1809 he showed why this works especially well: with
  independent Gaussian errors of equal spread, the least-squares fit is the
  most likely fit.
- **1913 — Markov chains.** Andrey Markov famously used his earlier chain models
  to study the order of vowels and consonants in a poem. The key idea: once you
  know the current state, earlier states don't add anything to the prediction
  of the next one. For a simple robot motion model, the current pose and speed,
  plus the next motor command, can be enough to predict the next pose without
  storing every place the robot has been.
- **1960 — The Kalman filter.** As computers made repeated updates practical,
  Rudolf Kalman published landmark work in 1960 and followed it with a paper
  co-authored with Richard Bucy in 1961. The work formalized **observability**:
  can you infer a hidden state from the measurements you have? It also gave an
  optimal way to estimate the state of a linear system with Gaussian noise. NASA
  used Kalman-filter methods on the **Ranger, Mariner, and Apollo** programs. On
  **Apollo 11**, the lunar module used noisy landing-radar readings to update
  its estimate of altitude and velocity above the Moon; the module's separate
  abort guidance system used Kalman-filter equations for rendezvous-radar data.

### From mathematical models to robots you can build

- **1970 — The Bayes filter.** Andrew Jazwinski's work on filtering helped
  establish the more general recipe: predict what happens next, then use
  Bayes' theorem to revise that prediction with new evidence. Repeat every
  time new data arrives. This setup also covers nonlinear motion and uncertainty
  that doesn't look like a bell curve.
- **2002 — Thrun's tutorial.** Sebastian Thrun brought these ideas to a wider
  computing audience with [*Probabilistic Robotics*](https://doi.org/10.1145/504729.504754),
  published in *Communications of the ACM*, March 2002, vol. 45, no. 3, pp. 52–57.
- **2004–2005 — DARPA Grand Challenges.** No vehicle finished the 2004 autonomous
  race across the desert. In 2005 Stanford's **Stanley** won, using probabilistic
  localization and perception to estimate where it was and which terrain it
  could drive over.
- **2007–2010 — ROS and open-source localization.** The first public code for
  **ROS** (Robot Operating System) appeared in 2007, and Willow Garage helped
  grow it into an open-source toolkit. By 2009–2010, the ROS navigation stack
  included **AMCL** (Adaptive Monte Carlo Localization), putting reusable
  particle-based localization within reach of robot builders.

### 1. The proverbial glass

Is the glass half full or half empty? This isn't a philosophy class: an
engineer wants to know **how much water is actually there, and how sure we
are**.

<Figure
  src="/intro-to-localization/glass-half-full.webp"
  alt="A straight-sided drinking glass with a waterline roughly halfway up."
  caption={<>It looks about half full. How close to 50% would you bet? Photo: <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Glass-of-water.jpg">Derek Jensen (Tysto)</a> (public domain).</>}
  width="36%"
/>

Here's how that answer can get more useful:

1. <Reveal>**“About 50% full.”** A best guess, but no idea how far off it might be.</Reveal>
2. <Reveal>**“Between 49% and 51% full.”** An interval, but no sense of how much to trust it.</Reveal>
3. <Reveal>**“Between 49% and 51%, with 90% confidence.”** Now we can weigh the risk of being outside the interval.</Reveal>
4. <Reveal>**“Between 49% and 51%, with 90% confidence, Gaussian.”** Now we've named the shape of our uncertainty: a bell curve.</Reveal>

<Reveal> Here, “90% confidence” means our model assigns 90% probability to the fill
level being in that interval. The shape lets us compare other ranges, too. How
likely is a fill level between 51% and 53% compared with one between 49% and 51%?</Reveal>

</Slide>
<Slide>

### 2. Why uncertainty changes what a robot does

No sensor measures with perfect accuracy, and no motor or other actuator moves
with perfect precision. We model uncertain quantities, such as a distance
reading or how far a drive command will move the robot, as **random variables**:
numbers that could take different values, with some values more likely than others.

A 60 cm-wide robot faces a 70 cm-wide doorway. The robot *estimates* that it's
centered and pointed straight through. If that's true, it has just **5 cm of
clearance on each side**. What if its position estimate is off by 8 cm?

<Figure
  src="/intro-to-localization/doorway-clearance.svg"
  alt="Top-down views of a 60 cm robot in a 70 cm doorway: centered with 5 cm on each side, or shifted 8 cm to the right and overlapping the jamb by 3 cm."
  caption="Both poses can be plausible from the same noisy reading. Only the centered one fits."
/>

Depending on the quality of its measurements and the cost of being wrong, the
robot can:

- <Reveal>**Go through** and accept the estimated risk if the margin looks good enough.</Reveal>
- <Reveal>**Decline to move** if it can't make the crossing safely.</Reveal>
- <Reveal>**Take the long way** through a wider opening it can locate more reliably.</Reveal>
- <Reveal>**Measure first:** stop and scan both jambs before deciding. Moving a sensor or changing viewpoint to get better information is called **active perception**.</Reveal>

</Slide>
<Slide>

### 3. Two ways to represent uncertainty

A robot could be on either side of a hallway with repeating doors. How should
its software store those possible locations?

| Representation | What the robot stores | When it's useful |
|---|---|---|
| **Parametric** | <Reveal>A named distribution and a few numbers that describe it. For a Gaussian, those are a mean (the center) and a covariance (the spread and how directions vary together).</Reveal> | <Reveal>One reasonably compact cluster of likely positions.</Reveal> |
| **Non-parametric (Monte Carlo)** | <Reveal>A cloud of **particles**, each representing a possible pose with an **importance weight**. A pose whose predicted sensor readings better match the real ones can receive more weight.</Reveal> | <Reveal>Several plausible positions at once, such as two distinct doorways that look alike.</Reveal> |

Think of the bell curve as a compact description of one hill. Particles are
like weighted pins on a map: they can bunch up on one hill or split across
several. **Particle filters**, including AMCL, update those pins as the robot
moves and gathers evidence.

<Figure
  src="/intro-to-localization/gaussian-vs-particles.svg"
  alt="A single smooth bell curve beside two clusters of particles, with larger dots representing more weight."
  caption="A single Gaussian summarizes one cluster; a particle cloud can keep two distinct guesses alive."
/>

</Slide>

### 4. Events, probability, and random variables

Roll a fair six-sided die. The whole box below shows every outcome. The
bubbles group the outcomes we want to ask about. Rolling a 3 also counts as
rolling an odd number, so the smaller bubble fits entirely inside the larger one.

<Figure
  src="/intro-to-localization/die-sample-space.svg"

  caption="A₁ is ‘roll a 3’; A₂ is ‘roll an odd number.’ Every outcome in A₁ is also in A₂."
/>

Now give each event a number that says **how likely it is**. On a fair die,
three of the six outcomes are odd, so A₂ points to 1/2 on the probability line.

<Figure

  alt="Four arrows map events to a probability number line. The empty event maps to 0, A₁ to 1/6, A₂ to 1/2, and the full sample space Ω to 1."
  caption="Probability maps a whole event to a value from 0 to 1. The impossible event has P(∅) = 0; the certain event has P(Ω) = 1."
/>

</Slide>

#### Give every outcome a number

A **random variable** answers a different question: what number should we
record for the outcome that actually happened? For example, use `X = 1` for
an odd roll and `X = 0` for an even one. The roll of 3 maps to 1, even though
the probability of rolling a 3 is 1/6.

<Figure
  src="/intro-to-localization/random-variable-mapping.svg"
  alt="Two sets labeled Ω and ℝ. The die outcomes 1, 3, and 5 point to the real number 1, while 2, 4, and 6 point to 0."
  caption="X assigns a value to each outcome in Ω. The output set ℝ is the real numbers, but this particular X only uses 0 and 1."
/>

</Slide>
<Slide>

#### From the diagrams to the notation

For a six-sided die, the **sample space** Ω is the set of all possible
outcomes: 1 through 6. An **event** is a set of outcomes you care about:
“roll a 3” is one event; “roll an odd number” includes 1, 3, and 5. A
probability assigns each event a number in `[0, 1]`.

- `P(Ω) = 1`: some outcome in the sample space must happen.
- `P(∅) = 0`: the empty event can't happen.
- If events A and B are **disjoint** (they can't both happen), then
  `P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B)`. So on a fair die, the chance of a 1 or a 3 is 2/6.
  “Roll a 3” and “roll an odd number” aren't disjoint: adding them would count 3 twice.

Three common ways to calculate probability are **counting** equally likely
outcomes (combinatorics), **measuring** a fraction of a uniform area or volume
(geometry), and **adding up area** under a probability density function
(calculus). The right method depends on how the outcomes are distributed.

A **random variable** assigns a number to each outcome. For example, X might
be the number of wheel ticks counted in a tenth of a second, or the actual
distance the robot travels in that time.

- **Discrete:** A tick count takes separate values: 0, 1, 2, and so on. For each
  value x that can occur with positive probability, `0 < P(X = x) ≤ 1`.
  Adding all the individual probabilities gives 1.
- **Continuous:** Model a distance measured with a ruler as able to take any real
  value between marks, even if the ruler only shows millimeters. Instead of
  asking for the chance of landing on one exact value (zero in a continuous
  model), ask for a range. **Cumulative probability** `P(X < x)` is the area
  under the **probability density function** (PDF) `f(x)` to the left of x.
  The curve's height is a density, not the probability of an exact reading.

<Figure
  src="/intro-to-localization/metric-ruler.webp"

  caption={<>The marks are discrete; a real length can fall anywhere between them. Photo: <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ruler_4scales_details.jpg">Tangopaso</a> (public domain).</>}
/>

### 5. Mean, variance, and standard deviation

Suppose a robot commanded to drive 1 m is equally likely to travel 0.98 m or
1.02 m. Its **expected value** `E[X]`, or mean μ, is 1.00 m. That's the center
of the guesses, not a promise that any run lands there.

**Variance** `V[X]` is the average *squared* distance from the mean. Squaring
stops misses on opposite sides from canceling out. In our example both misses
are 0.02 m from the center, so the variance is 0.0004 m². **Standard deviation**
`σ[X] = √(V[X])` brings the spread back into the original units: 0.02 m, or 2 cm.

<Callout type="info" title="The same idea in symbols">

| Quantity | Discrete: add over possible x values | Continuous: add up area over all x |
|---|---|---|
| Mean | `μ = E[X] = ∑ x · P(X = x)` | `μ = E[X] = ∫ x · f(x) dx` |
| Variance | `V[X] = ∑ (x − μ)² · P(X = x)` | `V[X] = ∫ (x − μ)² · f(x) dx` |
| Standard deviation | `σ[X] = √(V[X])` | `σ[X] = √(V[X])` |

No calculus needed here: read ∫ as “add up very thin slices.” In each column,
the mean averages positions; the variance averages squared misses from the mean.

</Callout>

</Slide>

### 6. Gaussians in one and two dimensions

Two sensors estimate that the left jamb is 35 cm from the robot's center. One
is usually within a centimeter or two; the other often misses by several
centimeters. Same estimate, different trust. When we model an uncertain
measurement as a **1D Gaussian**, the model is a **(μ, σ) pair**: μ says where
it's centered, and σ says how spread out it is. A larger σ puts more probability
farther from the center, so we're less confident that the true value is close to μ.

`f(x) = [1 / (σ√(2π))] · exp(−(x − μ)² / (2σ²))`

The `exp` part makes the curve fall away on both sides of μ. Increasing σ makes
the bell wider and lower, but the total area stays 1.

<Figure
  src="/intro-to-localization/gaussian-pdf.svg"
  alt="Bell curve with a dashed center line at 35 cm, shading from 32 to 38 cm, and red example distance readings on the horizontal axis."
  caption="The shaded interval is one standard deviation on each side of the mean (σ = 3 cm), containing about 68% of the modeled probability. The red dots are example measurements."
/>

</Slide>
<Slide>

#### From a bell curve to a cloud of points

On a map we care about two coordinates, **(x, y)**. Each has its own variance,
and the errors can move together. For instance, if a robot is driving northeast
and underestimates how far it drove, its actual position may be both farther
east and farther north than predicted. **Correlation** ρ describes that tendency:
a positive ρ means above-average x and above-average y tend to occur together.

<Callout type="info" title="Reading a 2 × 2 covariance matrix">

For the robot's position `(x, y)`, put the two standard deviations `σₓ` and
`σᵧ` and the correlation `ρ` together in one **covariance matrix**:

```text
    ⎡ σₓ²       ρ σₓ σᵧ ⎤
Σ = ⎣ ρ σₓ σᵧ   σᵧ²     ⎦
```

- **On the diagonal:** `σₓ²` (top left) and `σᵧ²` (bottom right) are the variances. Each tells you how spread out the uncertainty is along that map axis on its own.
- **Off the diagonal:** both entries are `ρ σₓ σᵧ`, the covariance between x and y. They capture how the coordinates vary together. If `ρ = 0`, both entries are zero and the coordinates are uncorrelated.

</Callout>

</Slide>
<Slide>

### 7. Uncertainty ellipses on a robot map

A cloud of dots can clutter a map. For a 2D Gaussian, an **uncertainty ellipse**
shows a region that contains a chosen share of the possible positions under
that model. Its **center** is the mean position. The **long axis** points where
we know the least; the **short axis** points where our estimate is tighter.
The **covariance matrix** stores both axis variances and how x and y vary
together. Its **eigenvectors** give those ellipse directions, and the square
roots of its **eigenvalues** give the standard deviations along them.

<Figure
  src="/intro-to-localization/uncertainty-ellipse.svg"
  alt="Tilted scatter cloud with two nested probability ellipses and perpendicular arrows along the long and short axes. Solid 3-sigma contour encloses 98.9%; dashed 3.41-sigma contour encloses 99.7%."
  caption="The arrows show the directions of greatest and least spread. The dashed ellipse extends beyond 3σ to enclose 99.7% of the modeled 2D positions."
/>

<Callout type="tip" title="Three sigma: check the dimension">

In **1D**, within 3 standard deviations of a Gaussian mean covers about
**99.7%** of the probability. For a **2D** Gaussian ellipse with semiaxes
`3√λ`, coverage is about **98.9%**. To enclose about 99.7% in 2D, use semiaxes
of about `3.41√λ` instead. Either way, an ellipse is not a guarantee that the
robot is inside it.

</Callout>

<details>
<summary>Optional Linear Algebra Deep Dive</summary>

If A is the position covariance matrix, an eigenvector **v** is a nonzero
arrow that A stretches or shrinks without turning it. The equation is
`λv = Av`, where **λ** is the scale factor, called the eigenvalue. Use the two
perpendicular eigenvector directions as the ellipse axes. Along each axis,
`√λ` is one standard deviation, so the semiaxis length is `k√λ` for a common
scale factor k chosen for the desired coverage.

</details>

### 8. Stretch: How uncertainty changes when we do math

Wheel ticks aren't a map position. A robot converts ticks to distance, then
uses distance and heading to update `(x, y)`. If the heading is a little off,
a long drive can push the estimated position far to one side. It's not enough
to convert the *best guess*; the robot needs to convert the *spread*, too.

<Callout type="info" title="Sneak Peek: How Robots Update Uncertainty">

For a **linear** conversion `y = Ax`, the covariance transforms as
`Σᵧ = A Σₓ Aᵀ`. Here x and y stand for the input and output quantities,
Σ is a covariance, and `Aᵀ` is A with its rows and columns swapped. Think of A
as a rule that stretches, turns, or combines directions in the uncertainty cloud.
For example, doubling a distance doubles its standard deviation and quadruples
its variance.

<details>
<summary>Step-by-Step Math Derivation</summary>

Here `x` and `y` are whole column vectors, and `A` is a fixed matrix.
`E[…]` means an average over possible outcomes, and `μₓ` and `μᵧ` are the mean vectors.

1. **Start with the covariance of `y`.** Subtract the mean, then multiply that column by its transpose to get the products of all pairs of deviations.

   ```text
   Σᵧ = E[(y − μᵧ)(y − μᵧ)ᵀ]
   ```

2. **Use linearity of expectation.** Applying the same fixed `A` to every input means we can apply it after averaging instead. Then substitute `y = Ax` and `μᵧ = Aμₓ`.

   ```text
   μᵧ = E[Ax] = A E[x] = Aμₓ
   Σᵧ = E[(Ax − Aμₓ)(Ax − Aμₓ)ᵀ]
   ```

3. **Factor out `A` from each difference.** Both terms in each difference were multiplied by the same matrix.

   ```text
   Σᵧ = E[(A(x − μₓ))(A(x − μₓ))ᵀ]
   ```

4. **Transpose the product in reverse order.** The rule `(MN)ᵀ = NᵀMᵀ` moves the transposed `A` to the far right.

   ```text
   Σᵧ = E[A(x − μₓ)(x − μₓ)ᵀ Aᵀ]
   ```

5. **Take the constant matrices outside the expectation.** `A` and `Aᵀ` stay the same for every outcome.

   ```text
   Σᵧ = A E[(x − μₓ)(x − μₓ)ᵀ] Aᵀ
   ```

6. **Recognize the covariance of `x`.** The average between the two fixed matrices is exactly `Σₓ`.

   ```text
   Σᵧ = A Σₓ Aᵀ
   ```

</details>

Real motion can be **nonlinear**: a change in heading sends a robot along an
arc, not a straight line. Near the current best guess, we can approximate
`y = f(x)` with a linear rule. Its **Jacobian** J tells us how a small change
in each input changes the outputs. That gives `Σᵧ ≈ J Σₓ Jᵀ`. This describes
how existing uncertainty changes; new noise from the motion itself also needs
its own allowance.

The next step is **Bayes and Kalman filters**: predict where the robot moved
and how uncertain that prediction is, then use new measurements to revise both.

</Callout>
