<Section id="joint-and-conditional" n="01" title="Joint & Conditional Probability" chip="Given what?">

<Slide>

“Statistically, the odds of being in a commercial airplane crash are roughly
**1 in 11,000,000 — super safe!**”

Now add: “You're on a plane, and **both engines just caught fire**.” Would you
still use the same odds? Of course not. You have learned something important
about *this particular flight*. The baseline odds no longer describe your situation.

<Figure
  src="/bayes-filter/airplane-conditional.svg"
  alt="Two panels show the same jet cruising peacefully, then with flames and smoke from both engines. New evidence changes the applicable odds; the quoted baseline is illustrative, not a current risk estimate."
  caption="Same airplane, different information. The quoted 1-in-11-million figure is only the hook — see the caveat below, not a calculation of flight safety."
/>

<Callout type="info" title="A hook, not an aviation risk calculation">

The widely repeated “1 in 11 million” claim above is an illustration, not a
precisely defined current crash statistic. Risk depends on the event, time
period, and population. For comparison, [MIT's 2024 report](https://news.mit.edu/2024/study-flying-keeps-getting-safer-0807)
estimates **fatality risk**, not crash risk, at about **1 in 13.7 million passenger
boardings worldwide in 2018–2022**. We won't invent a number for the engine-fire
scenario. The lesson is simply that evidence changes which odds apply.

</Callout>

A robot faces the same reasoning problem on a smaller scale: “Doors in this
hallway are usually open” is different from “This door is open **given that**
my camera sees a reflection across the doorway.” **Conditional probability**
lets us express that difference.

</Slide>
<Slide>

### Both happening vs. one happening given the other

Let **X** be a hidden state, such as whether a door is open, and **Z** be a
sensor reading. Lowercase **x** and **z** mean particular values: for example,
`x = open` and `z = camera says Open`. Capitalized `Open` and `Closed` below
are **camera labels**, not guarantees about reality.

| Idea | Formula | Plain English |
|---|---|---|
| **Joint probability** | `P(x, z) = P(X = x, Z = z)` | The chance that the state is x **and** the reading is z, together. |
| **Conditional probability** | `P(x ∣ z) = P(x, z) / P(z)` | Among cases where we observed z, what fraction have state x? Requires `P(z) > 0`. |
| **Independence** | `P(x, z) = P(x)P(z)` and `P(x ∣ z) = P(x)` | Learning z tells us nothing about x. |

The vertical bar **∣** means **“given.”** Imagine shrinking your whole sample
space to just the cases matching the observation; dividing by `P(z)` rescales
that smaller world to a total probability of 1.

<Figure
  src="/bayes-filter/shrinking-universe.svg"
  alt="Left: events X and Z overlap inside the full sample space. Right: Z is enlarged and becomes the entire universe; the overlap with X occupies the same fraction of Z. Joint probability uses the whole sample space as its denominator, while conditional probability uses only Z."
  caption="Keep the overlap; change the denominator. These areas are schematic, not the probabilities from the door example."
/>

For instance, `P(door open, camera says Open)` counts both events together.
`P(door open ∣ camera says Open)` asks how much to trust that reading.

A coin flip unrelated to the door should not change our door estimate: those
events are independent. A useful camera is **not** independent of the door
state — otherwise it couldn't help us!

</Slide>

</Section>

<Section id="belief-and-markov" n="02" title="Belief, Prediction & the Markov Assumption" chip="Remember uncertainty">

<Slide>

### The robot keeps a distribution, not a verdict

In [Module 1](/courses/intro-to-localization), a robot's pose could be uncertain.
Instead of pretending it knows the exact state, our estimator keeps a **belief**:
a probability distribution over possible states. Here we'll estimate just
**door open** or **door closed** so we can see every calculation. In localization,
the possibilities would instead be robot positions and headings.

A belief `[0.6, 0.4]` means “60% probability open, 40% closed,” **not** “the door
is 60% of the way open.” Our model only has two possible states.

Let `t` count steps, starting with the initial state `x₀`. An action **uₜ is
applied at state xₜ**, taking the system toward `xₜ₊₁`; measurement **zₜ describes
state xₜ**. So the action leading into `xₜ` is **uₜ₋₁**, not uₜ.

```text
x₀ ──u₀──→ x₁ ──u₁──→ x₂ ── … ──uₜ₋₁──→ xₜ
            ↓ z₁        ↓ z₂                 ↓ zₜ
```

The previous states run from `x₀` through `xₜ₋₁`, and the actions taken so far
run from `u₀` through `uₜ₋₁`. Measurements run from `z₁` through `zₜ`.
A colon such as `z₁:ₜ` means **all measurements from step 1 to t**.
We start with an initial prior `bel(x₀)`.

```text
bel(xₜ) = P(xₜ ∣ z₁:ₜ, u₀:ₜ₋₁)
```

This **posterior belief** describes the state *after* using the latest
measurement. It includes the evidence from all measurements and actions so far;
the earlier states are uncertain, not extra known values we condition on.

```text
b̄el(xₜ) = P(xₜ ∣ z₁:ₜ₋₁, u₀:ₜ₋₁)
```

This **predicted belief** describes the same state xₜ after action uₜ₋₁,
**before** measurement zₜ. The bar above `bel` is our “prediction only” flag.
At step 1, the measurement history `z₁:₀` is empty: predict from `bel(x₀)`
using u₀, then update with z₁.

</Slide>
<Slide>

### Why not replay the robot's entire life?

Suppose a robot already knows its current position and speed. To predict the
next position, it shouldn't need to replay last week's wheel commands.
The **Markov assumption** says that a sufficiently complete current state
contains all history relevant to what happens next. Prediction then needs only
the previous state and the newest action; the sensor model needs only the
current state.

But the robot **doesn't know which previous state is true**. It keeps the
previous **belief** as a compact summary and combines predictions from every
possible previous state. That is why a recursive filter can carry yesterday's
information forward without storing every raw reading.

<Figure
  src="/bayes-filter/markov-timeline.svg"
  alt="A chain of states x t−2, x t−1, x t, x t+1 has controls leading from each departure state into the next state and sensor readings below each same-index state. Older history is faded. Previous state x t−1, incoming action u t−1, and latest reading z t are highlighted for estimating x t."
  caption="Our indexing: uₜ₋₁ leads into xₜ, and uₜ leads toward xₜ₊₁. The previous state summarizes relevant history; the filter carries a belief over that unknown state."
/>

The arrows show **model dependencies**, not certainty. Once a previous state
is specified, earlier states add nothing to this motion model. Since we don't
know that previous state, prediction still averages over its possible values.

<Callout type="info" title="Under the Hood: models are not beliefs">

The motion model is `P(xₜ ∣ xₜ₋₁, uₜ₋₁)`: how likely is a new state if the previous
state and action are specified? The measurement model is `P(zₜ ∣ xₜ)`.
For discrete states, the actual filter is:

```text
Predict: b̄el(xₜ) = Σ over xₜ₋₁ [P(xₜ ∣ xₜ₋₁, uₜ₋₁) bel(xₜ₋₁)]
Update:   bel(xₜ) = η P(zₜ ∣ xₜ) b̄el(xₜ)
```

**Predict:** for each possible old state, multiply “chance of getting here” by
“chance we were there,” then add those paths. **Update:** multiply that
prediction by how well the latest reading fits, then normalize.

Don't equate `b̄el(xₜ)` with `P(xₜ ∣ xₜ₋₁, uₜ₋₁)` or equate `bel(xₜ)` with
`P(xₜ ∣ xₜ₋₁, zₜ, uₜ₋₁)`. Those right-hand expressions condition on a particular
previous state; the belief must account for **all** possible previous states.
If that previous state were known exactly, its belief would be 1 there and 0 elsewhere.

</Callout>

This is an assumption about our **model**, not a magic law. A moving robot may
need speed as well as position in its state. Our door model assumes nothing
else changes the door between steps. If a spring pulls it shut, we must model that too.

</Slide>

</Section>

<Section id="bayesian-inference" n="03" title="Bayesian Inference: Updating Our Belief" chip="Evidence × prior">

<Slide>

A camera reports `Open`. We need the probability the **door is open given the
reading**, but camera testing tells us the probability of a **reading given the
door state**. Those are different questions! Bayes' rule connects them:

```text
P(x ∣ z) = P(z ∣ x) P(x) / P(z) = η P(z ∣ x) P(x)
```

In words: **new belief = how well the reading fits × old belief, rescaled**.
For the full filter, the “old belief” at measurement time is the prediction `b̄el`.

| Piece | What it means for our robot |
|---|---|
| **Posterior** `P(x ∣ z)` | Our new knowledge about the state **after** incorporating the measurement. |
| **Measurement model / likelihood** `P(z ∣ x)` | How likely the camera is to produce reading z **if** the true state is x. It is not yet the probability of x! |
| **Prior** `P(x)` | What we believed **before** this measurement. |
| **Normalization factor** `η = 1 / P(z)` | A scaling constant that makes the probabilities add back up to **1**. Say “eta.” |

We don't need to calculate `P(z)` separately. Compute an unnormalized score for
each state, add the scores, then **divide each score by their sum**. That's
multiplication by η. It preserves their relative sizes. In the
[glass-door example below](#sec-glass-door-measurements), a before-and-after
bar diagram shows exactly how η stretches two scores to total 100%.

<Callout type="tip" title="Same reading, different meaning">

`P(camera says Open ∣ door open) = 0.9` does **not** mean
`P(door open ∣ camera says Open) = 0.9`. A false-open reading may be common,
and the prior matters. Let's calculate rather than guessing.

</Callout>

<Section id="glass-door-measurements" n="04" title="Worked Example 1: The Glass Door" chip="Measurements only">

<Slide>

### 1. Build the model by thinking about the camera

Your robot approaches a glass door. It can see the hallway beyond it — but
that doesn't prove the glass isn't in the way. Reflections and a hard-to-see
edge can fool its camera. For this **teaching model**, suppose camera testing gives:

- If the door is **closed**, the camera confuses it with open **half the time**.
- If the door is **open**, the camera correctly reports `Open` **90% of the time**.

These numbers are assumptions for our example, not measurements of the photographed door.

<Figure
  src="/bayes-filter/glass-door.webp"
  alt="A transparent glass entrance door at the Glass House Visitors Center, with reflections, lettering, and a vertical handle."
  caption={<>Glass can be difficult to distinguish from an opening. Photograph by <a href="https://christopherpeterson.com">Christopher Peterson</a>. <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Glass_house_philip_johnson_architecture.jpg">Source</a>, <a href="https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/3.0/">CC BY 3.0</a> (resized and converted to WebP).</>}
  width="48%"
/>

Our **measurement model** puts readings in **rows** and true states in **columns**:

| Camera reading z ↓ / True state x → | open | closed |
|---|---|---|
| `Open` | `P(Open ∣ open) = 0.9` | `P(Open ∣ closed) = 0.5` |
| `Closed` | `P(Closed ∣ open) = 0.1` | `P(Closed ∣ closed) = 0.5` |

Each **column** sums to 1 because it lists all possible readings for one true
state. A row doesn't have to sum to 1: its entries assume different true states.

For now, the robot takes measurements **without changing the door**. We assume
the door stays in the same state and readings are independent **given that state**.
Repeated copies of one frozen camera frame would not be fresh evidence.

</Slide>
<Slide>

### 2. Start at 50/50, then process the first `Open`

We have no reason to favor either state, so choose the **initial prior**:

```text
P(x = open) = 0.5       P(x = closed) = 0.5
```

Here 50/50 is a modeling choice for two states, not a universal definition of ignorance.
The camera reports `Open`. Score each possible state using the `Open` row:

```text
bel(open)   = η × 0.9 × 0.5 = 0.45η
bel(closed) = η × 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25η
```

The `0.9` and `0.5` say how well that reading fits each state; the other `0.5`
in each line is our prior. The scores are `0.45` and `0.25`, totaling `0.70`.
Normalize them:

```text
η = 1 / (0.45 + 0.25) ≈ 1.429
bel(open)   = 0.45 / 0.70 ≈ 0.643
bel(closed) = 0.25 / 0.70 ≈ 0.357
```

<Figure
  src="/bayes-filter/normalization.svg"
  alt="Before normalization, a stacked bar has open score 0.45 and closed score 0.25, totaling 0.70 below a dashed 1.0 line. After multiplying both by eta approximately 1.429, open is 0.643 and closed is 0.357, totaling 1.0. The open-to-closed ratio stays 9 to 5."
  caption="η changes the total, not the balance of evidence. Both stacked segments stretch by the same factor; values are rounded only for display."
/>

The new belief is **64.3% open, 35.7% closed**. An `Open` reading favors an
open door, but it isn't overwhelming evidence: closed glass doors produce that
same reading half the time!

<Callout type="warn" title="Pause & Decide">

**Is 64.3% confident enough to drive through a glass door?** How is this smarter
than `if camera == "Open": drive()`?

The filter keeps a **35.7% chance of a collision-causing closed door** visible
to the planner. It estimates; it doesn't choose a safe action for us. A planner
could stop, use a different sensor, or take another route. A probability alone
isn't a safety guarantee — the cost of being wrong matters.

</Callout>

</Slide>
<Slide>

### 3. Repeat the update in Python

<Figure
  src="/bayes-filter/elementwise-update.svg"
  alt="A two-by-two measurement model highlights the Open row [0.9, 0.5]. The prior column [0.5, 0.5] is labeled open then closed. Matching entries give open score 0.9 times 0.5 equals 0.45 and closed score 0.5 times 0.5 equals 0.25. A Closed reading would select row [0.1, 0.5] instead."
  caption="Select one row, then pair likelihood and prior for each state. This element-wise operation produces scores, not yet a normalized belief."
/>

Each posterior becomes the next prior. This **recursive update** carries the
earlier evidence forward in just two numbers. Run this with Python and NumPy
(`python -m pip install numpy` if needed):

<CodeTabs>
<CodeTab label="door_measurements.py">

```python
import numpy as np

# State order everywhere: [open, closed].
belief = np.array([0.5, 0.5])

# Rows: camera reading [Open, Closed]. Columns: true state [open, closed].
measurement_model = np.array([
    [0.9, 0.5],  # P(camera says Open | each true state)
    [0.1, 0.5],  # P(camera says Closed | each true state)
])

# Open, Open, Open, Open, Open, Closed, Open, Open
measurements = [0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 1, 0, 0]
labels = ["Open", "Closed"]

print("step  reading  bel(open)  bel(closed)")
for step, z in enumerate(measurements, start=1):
    # Select the likelihood row for the reading we just received.
    # * multiplies matching entries, NOT matrices:
    # [likelihood_open * prior_open, likelihood_closed * prior_closed].
    scores = measurement_model[z] * belief
    total = scores.sum()
    if total <= 0:
        raise ValueError("This reading is impossible under our model.")
    belief = scores / total  # Normalize; equivalent to multiplying by eta.
    print(f"{step:>4}  {labels[z]:>7}  {belief[0]:.3f}      {belief[1]:.3f}")
```

</CodeTab>
</CodeTabs>

For `z = 0`, we pick `[0.9, 0.5]`. For `z = 1`, we pick `[0.1, 0.5]`.
The `*` operator does **element-wise multiplication**: open is paired with open,
closed with closed. After normalizing, `belief` stores the updated distribution.
Keep full precision internally; round only when printing.

</Slide>
<Slide>

### 4. Watch the belief build — and wobble

| Step | Reading | bel(open) | bel(closed) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Initial prior | 0.500 | 0.500 |
| 1 | `Open` | 0.643 | 0.357 |
| 2 | `Open` | 0.764 | 0.236 |
| 3 | `Open` | 0.854 | 0.146 |
| 4 | `Open` | 0.913 | 0.087 |
| 5 | `Open` | 0.950 | 0.050 |
| 6 | `Closed` | 0.791 | 0.209 |
| 7 | `Open` | 0.872 | 0.128 |
| 8 | `Open` | 0.924 | 0.076 |

<Figure
  src="/bayes-filter/belief-convergence.svg"
  alt="Belief in open rises from 0.500 initially through 0.643, 0.764, 0.854, 0.913, and 0.950, dips to 0.791 at the sixth reading Closed, then recovers to 0.872 and 0.924."
  caption="Five Open readings build confidence. The sixth reading, Closed, lowers it — without erasing everything learned earlier."
/>

The `Closed` reading at step 6 is **possible even if the door is open**: its
likelihood there is 0.1. It favors closed over open by a factor of five
(`0.5 / 0.1`), so belief in open drops to **0.791**. The next two `Open` readings
bring it back up. We don't know whether reading 6 was wrong; the filter simply
weighs it alongside the earlier evidence.

With repeated `Open` evidence, this fixed-door model tends toward high confidence
in open. It does **not** prove the door is open, and confidence need not increase
at every step. A badly calibrated camera or correlated frames can make a filter
overconfident. Better arithmetic cannot rescue a bad model.

</Slide>

<Section id="full-bayes-filter" n="05" title="Worked Example 2: Add Actions" chip="Predict → Update">

<Slide>

### 1. What if the robot can push?

Now the robot has an actuator. Before looking again, it can **push** the door
or do **nothing**. A command is not proof of success: maybe the push wasn't
strong enough. We need an **action model**, also called a motion or transition
model, giving probabilities for the next state.

| Action | Previous state | Next: open | Next: closed |
|---|---|---|---|
| `push` | open | 1.0 | 0.0 |
| `push` | closed | 0.6 | 0.4 |
| `none` | open | 1.0 | 0.0 |
| `none` | closed | 0.0 | 1.0 |

In this simplified world, a push never closes an open door, and opens a closed
door **60% of the time**. With `none`, nothing changes. Each row here describes
one action and previous state, so its two outcome probabilities sum to 1.

### 2. Predict after pushing, before measuring

**Reset to a fresh 50/50 prior** `bel(x₀)` — don't continue from Example 1.
The first push is **u₀**, which predicts the door state **x₁**; the camera then
provides **z₁**. To find the chance of open after pushing, add two possible paths:
it was already open, or it was closed and the push succeeded.

```text
b̄el(open)   = 1.0 × 0.5 + 0.6 × 0.5 = 0.8
b̄el(closed) = 0.0 × 0.5 + 0.4 × 0.5 = 0.2
```

For each path, multiply its transition probability by our belief in its starting
state. Add the paths ending at the same destination. Our **predicted belief is
80% open, 20% closed**, before receiving any new camera evidence.

</Slide>
<Slide>

### 3. A matrix is just a tidy table of paths

Keep state order **[open, closed]**. Put **previous states in columns** and
**next states in rows**. Then the prediction becomes a matrix times a column vector:

```text
                          previous: open  closed
                                    ↓     ↓
                         M_push = [ 1.0   0.6 ]  ← next: open
                                  [ 0.0   0.4 ]  ← next: closed

                         M_none = [ 1.0   0.0 ]
                                  [ 0.0   1.0 ]

[ b̄el(open)   ]       [ bel(open)   ]
[ b̄el(closed) ] = M × [ bel(closed) ]

[ 0.8 ]   [ 1.0  0.6 ]   [ 0.5 ]
[ 0.2 ] = [ 0.0  0.4 ] × [ 0.5 ]
```

**Row × column** means multiply matching entries and add: the top row gives
`1.0 × 0.5 + 0.6 × 0.5`. The bottom row gives
`0.0 × 0.5 + 0.4 × 0.5`. That's the arithmetic we already did — no new trick.

Each **column** of these matrices sums to 1, because the door has to end up
somewhere for each starting state. The `none` matrix is the **identity matrix**:
it leaves our belief unchanged.

<Callout type="tip" title="Two different kinds of multiplication">

In NumPy, prediction is `predicted = motion_model @ belief` — **matrix–vector**
multiplication, which adds paths. The sensor update is
`scores = measurement_model[z] * predicted` — **element-wise** multiplication,
which scores each state against the reading. Then normalize the scores.

</Callout>

</Slide>
<Slide>

### 4. Update with the camera

The camera now says `Open`. Use the **same sensor model** as before, but start
from the stronger predicted belief `[0.8, 0.2]`:

```text
bel(open)   = η × 0.9 × 0.8 = 0.72η
bel(closed) = η × 0.5 × 0.2 = 0.10η

η = 1 / (0.72 + 0.10) ≈ 1.2195
bel(open)   = 0.72 / 0.82 ≈ 0.878
bel(closed) = 0.10 / 0.82 ≈ 0.122
```

Again: likelihood × prediction, then divide by the total. Now we believe the
door is **87.8% likely to be open**.

| Starting from 50/50 | Before reading `Open` | After reading `Open` |
|---|---|---|
| Measurement alone (`none`) | 0.500 open | **0.643 open** |
| Push, then measurement | 0.800 open | **0.878 open** |

<Figure
  src="/bayes-filter/action-comparison.svg"
  alt="Grouped bars compare probability open across three stages. Measure only: 0.500 prior, 0.500 prediction with no action, 0.643 after an Open reading. Push and measure: 0.500 prior, 0.800 after push u zero, 0.878 after the same Open reading z one."
  caption="Same initial belief and sensor model. With no action the prediction stays 0.500; a push changes it to 0.800 before either track uses the Open reading."
/>

The camera didn't improve. The action changed the world and therefore changed
what we should believe **before** seeing the reading. An action can also make
uncertainty larger in other models; pushing is not a universal confidence boost.

</Slide>

### 5. The full Bayes filter: repeat two steps

1. **Predict:** use the latest action, motion model, and previous belief to
   distribute probability over the possible next states.
2. **Update:** use the latest measurement and sensor model to favor states that
   explain the reading, then normalize.
3. **Repeat:** today's posterior becomes tomorrow's starting belief.

For localization, replace door states with possible **robot poses**, a push with
a **drive command**, and the camera label with a **landmark or range reading**.
The map and the models let us predict which readings each pose would produce.
The same predict–update recipe still applies; only the state space and models change.

<Callout type="info" title="Under the Hood: what scales up?">

Our two-state filter is an exact discrete Bayes filter for the stated model.
For many discrete states, the sum has more terms. For continuous poses, it becomes
an integral over possible previous states. Kalman filters and particle filters
are ways to make versions of this recipe practical for different models and
belief representations. You don't need that heavier notation to understand the loop.

</Callout>

<Section id="check-your-understanding" n="06" title="Check Your Understanding" chip="Knowledge check">

<Quiz>
  <Q>
    <Prompt>A broken camera outputs `Open` or `Closed` with probability 0.5 each, regardless of the true door state. What does one reading do to the predicted belief?</Prompt>
    <Explain>Both state probabilities get multiplied by the same 0.5. Normalization cancels that shared factor, so the belief stays the same. This sensor provides no information about the state.</Explain>
    <Choice>Resets the belief to 50/50.</Choice>
    <Choice correct>Leaves the predicted belief unchanged.</Choice>
    <Choice>Makes the reported state certain.</Choice>
  </Q>
  <Q>
    <Prompt>Why do we need the normalization factor η after multiplying likelihoods by the prior?</Prompt>
    <Explain>The products are relative scores, not usually probabilities that sum to 1. Dividing by their sum rescales them without changing their ratio. If every score is zero, the model considers that reading impossible and we must handle the model mismatch rather than divide by zero.</Explain>
    <Choice>It always increases belief in open.</Choice>
    <Choice>It removes noisy readings from the history.</Choice>
    <Choice correct>It makes the updated state probabilities sum to 1.</Choice>
  </Q>
  <Q>
    <Prompt>Starting from 50/50, a push makes the predicted belief 80% open before the camera is read. Why?</Prompt>
    <Explain>The door might already be open (0.5), or it might be closed and successfully pushed open (0.5 × 0.6 = 0.3). Adding those paths gives 0.8. The action model predicts outcomes; sending a command doesn't guarantee success.</Explain>
    <Choice correct>We add the paths that end open, weighted by their starting beliefs.</Choice>
    <Choice>A push guarantees the door is open.</Choice>
    <Choice>The filter secretly used the next camera reading.</Choice>
  </Q>
</Quiz>

**Take it with you:** don't replace uncertainty with a yes/no guess too early.
Carry a belief, **predict → update**, and let the planner decide what level of
risk is acceptable.
